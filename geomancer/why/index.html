Why your imported terrain has no rivers

Last updated 9 August 2026

You export a heightmap out of Gaea, or World Creator, or whatever you sculpt in. It looks fantastic. There are valleys cut into it, branching down off the high ground exactly the way real ones do, because an erosion simulation spent a few million iterations putting them there.

You import it into Unreal. The valleys are still there. The water isn't.

So you open the Water plugin and start drawing river splines by hand, down valleys that were carved by water in the first place. Somewhere around the fourth or fifth one you notice you're guessing — this one probably joins that one, this one probably reaches the sea, this one just sort of stops. And a river that just sort of stops is the thing that makes a landscape look built rather than found.

The information was thrown away before you got here

A heightmap is a picture of height. That is the whole format. One value per point, how far up the ground is, and nothing else.

Your erosion tool knew far more than that. To carve those valleys it had to work out where rain landed, which way it ran, where it pooled, where it merged, how much of it there was by the time it reached the bottom. That simulation is the reason the terrain looks right.

Then you exported, and all of it was discarded except the final shape. The valleys are the fossil evidence of water. The water itself never made it into the file.

So when you sit down in Unreal to draw rivers, you aren't authoring something new. You are reconstructing, by eye and by hand, information that existed an hour ago and got flattened out of the export. The terrain in front of you already implies exactly where the water goes. You're just the one who has to work it out again.

Your options, honestly

Draw the splines by hand. It works, and for a small hero area it is genuinely the right call — you get complete control over every bend. It stops being the right call somewhere around the point where you're drawing your twentieth river and can no longer remember whether the fourth one had an outlet.

Go back and export more than height. Some terrain tools will give you flow maps or wetness masks alongside the heightmap. If yours does, that data is worth having. You'll still be converting it into actual water bodies yourself, and you'll be doing it again every time the terrain changes.

Use a dedicated water system. There are excellent ones, and they solve a different problem: how water looks and behaves once it's there — the shading, the flow, the simulation. They are not trying to work out where it belongs. If you already use one, this isn't a competitor to it.

Or have something read the terrain and work it out. Which is the one I ended up building, though not for the reason you'd assume.

That isn't why I built it

I didn't come to this from a heightmap.

I came to it because I find worldbuilding hard.

I was working alone. I wanted a way to lay out lakes and rivers that didn't mean placing every one by hand — it eats the time I'd rather spend on design, it gets tedious, and automating it looked like the way to get better results, not just faster ones. And underneath that was the thing I always hit, which is that a blank landscape is a blank page. Give me something that isn't quite right yet and I'll keep at it until it is. Give me an empty valley and ask me where the river should go, and I will stare at it, put something down, decide it looks wrong, and move it, and still not know whether it's right.

That's not modesty and it isn't a complaint. It's just the shape of what I'm good at. I'd guess a fair number of solo developers reading this recognise it, because the part of this job that stops you is rarely the part you expected.

What I wanted was for the world to meet me halfway. Not to generate something for me — I didn't want a random world, I wanted a world that looks and feels real at any scale. I wanted to point at terrain I'd made and have the water turn up where the terrain said it should.

So I started writing it. And it turned out the terrain really does know: everything needed to place water correctly is already in the heightmap, if you're willing to do the analysis the erosion tool did and nobody bothered to keep.

Then it kept growing. Water was meant to be a first step and became the proof that the approach works at all, which is why it's the part that ships first. Biomes, flora and erosion read the same analysis, and they come in that order because it's the order a world assembles in: water decides where life can be, life decides what covers the ground, and time decides what all of it has been through.

Which is the thing I'm actually chasing. A world where every part is a consequence of another one — because that's the difference between a landscape that was laid out and one that looks lived in.

How it actually works

Three questions, in order. Each one is a well-studied problem in terrain hydrology, and the names are worth knowing if you want to read further.

Where does water collect? Any dip in the ground could hold water, and a raw heightmap has thousands of them — mostly single-pixel noise from the export, not lakes. So you flood the terrain from its edges inward and watch what fills, which separates real basins from artefacts and tells you which depressions have an outlet and which don't. A closed basin with no outlet is an inland sea; one that spills is a lake with a river leaving it. This is priority-flood depression filling.

Where does water become a river? A trickle isn't a river. What makes one is how much ground drains through a single point — so you route every cell of the landscape downhill, add up how much upslope terrain feeds each point, and rivers begin where that total crosses a threshold. High on a ridge, nothing. Down where a dozen slopes converge, a lot. That is flow accumulation, and it's why the rivers turn up in the valleys rather than being drawn there.

How do rivers combine? Not the way you'd guess. Two streams of the same size meeting make a bigger one — but a small stream joining a large one doesn't change the large one at all, which is exactly how it behaves in the world. Numbering the network that way, from the headwaters down, means every reach knows how much of the system is behind it, so width and depth grow with the catchment instead of being set by hand. That's Strahler ordering.

Which brings it back to the river that just sort of stops. Once depressions are filled, every point on the map has somewhere downhill to go — to the sea, to the edge of the map, or to a lake with a spill point that then carries on. A dead end isn't something the tool tries to avoid; it's something the analysis can't produce. That's the difference between placing rivers and deriving them.

Then the results are placed as native Unreal water body actors — the same ones you'd have drawn yourself. Not a proprietary water system, not a black box: ordinary actors, editable in the standard Details panel, that work with everything already built on top of Epic's water. If you don't like where a river went, drag it.

None of these three are my inventions, and that's the point. They're the standard approach in terrain hydrology, worked out by people who study rivers for a living — the priority-flood and flow-accumulation pairing follows Barnes et al. (2014). What I did was apply them to a landscape actor and have the results come out as engine water bodies. I'd rather tell you it's textbook than sell you magic; textbook means the results are principled, and it means you can go and read why.

What it doesn't do

It doesn't generate terrain. It reads the landscape you already have — bring your own heightmap, sculpt, or terrain tool. It's editor-only, so nothing compiles into your packaged build. It doesn't do waterfalls, and it assumes one surface height per point, so caves and overhangs are outside what the analysis can see.

The full documentation is public and free to read before you buy any of it — every setting, every warning, and a longer list of the edges than this one.
